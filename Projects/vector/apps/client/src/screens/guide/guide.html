The job

You are the controller for a TRACON and its Center airspace 150 NM out, from the surface to FL450: New York (N90: Kennedy, LaGuardia and Newark), Chicago (C90: O’Hare and Midway) or Dallas–Fort Worth (D10: DFW and Love Field). They all work exactly the same way; the examples in this handbook are from New York, and Chicago and Dallas–Fort Worth have their own sections.

Where your airspace is. The dashed circle on the scope is the edge of everything you control: 150 NM around the airports, surface to FL450. Inside it, the solid light-blue outline labeled with the TRACON (N90, C90 or D10 TRACON) is the real approach and departure area, traced from the FAA’s minimum vectoring altitude chart for it. Beyond the TRACON is the Center’s airspace (New York, Chicago or Fort Worth Center), which you also work in Vector. Both are map layers you can turn off.

Three kinds of traffic come your way, and each has a different goal:

Arrivals — descend them from cruise, sequence them and clear them for the ILS.
Departures — release them for takeoff, climb them to their requested level and hand them to Center.
Overflights — keep them separated and hand them to Center before they leave.

Every aircraft is controlled through the command menus. There are no text commands, and the keyboard only runs the game (pause, speed, panels, zoom), never an aircraft. Pilots read back every instruction a few seconds after you send it, just as they would on a real frequency.

Everything is built on real data: FAA coded procedures (STARs, SIDs, ILS approaches and airways), NASR frequencies and Center boundaries, the TRACONs’ minimum vectoring altitude charts, Center minimum IFR altitudes, Class B and C airspace, and US Census shoreline.

Starting a session

Sign in, choose New session on an airspace’s card, and set the session up.

The setup screen

  • Traffic. Pick Easy, Normal, Hard or Expert, and the airline mix. Traffic is set for the whole session. You can fine-tune the rates for practice, but only the four difficulties count for the records.
  • Wind & runways. Live (the default) uses the real current weather at each of your airports, shown with each airport’s report; Random wind is realistic for the region; New wind rolls another day. Manual wind lets you set direction and speed. The preview shows each airport’s wind and the runways it will use, and you can override the runway configuration per airport (unsuitable choices are flagged).
  • Rules & realism. Separation minima, approach criteria, pilot behavior, radar, Center automation and RP values. The defaults are realistic; change them only if you want to.

The session starts with about five minutes of traffic already flowing, so the scope isn’t empty. Press Space to pause at any time, and use the 1× 2× 4× buttons (or , and .) to change sim speed.

Chicago

C90, the Chicago TRACON: O’Hare and Midway, 15 NM apart beside Lake Michigan, with Chicago Center around you. Everything in this handbook works the same way there.

O’Hare

Six parallel east–west runways and two diagonals, every one with an ILS but 04L. With a wind from the west O’Hare runs a triple west flow, landing 27L, 28C and 27R and departing 28R and 22L; from the east, the east flow lands 10C, 09R and 09L and departs 10L and 04L. When the crosswind is too much on one of the three, it drops to two arrival runways; a strong southwest or northeast wind puts it on the 22s or the 4s. Arrivals are spread across every arrival runway in use, so you run two or three finals at once: keep each final’s traffic on its own localizer and watch the spacing on each.

Its traffic is the United and American hubs, with their regional partners (SkyWest, Envoy, Republic, PSA), Delta to its hubs, Alaska, Frontier and Air Canada, Aeromexico to Mexico City, FedEx and UPS freighters, and long-haul carriers to their own hubs: British Airways and Virgin Atlantic, Air France, Lufthansa, KLM, Swiss, Aer Lingus, LOT, Turkish, Qatar, Emirates, Air India, and ANA and Japan Airlines to Tokyo.

Midway

Mostly Southwest 737s, with Delta and Endeavor to Delta’s hubs. Midway’s ILS runways are 31R, 13L and 04R. Its 22L has no ILS (real arrivals land there on an RNAV or visual approach, which Vector doesn’t model), so in a southwest wind Midway lands 31R and departs 22L.

What’s different from New York

  • Arrivals descend via. Eleven of Chicago’s 14 STARs publish altitude restrictions, some with speeds (FYTTE7: STNLE 280 kt, CLSBY 270 kt, FYTTE at 11,000 ft and 250 kt), so most arrivals check in “descending via” and fly them on their own until you give them an altitude.
  • You vector every departure. O’Hare’s and Midway’s real departure procedures are radar-vector SIDs published only as text, so none are in the FAA’s coded data. Departures fly runway heading to 5,000 ft and expect your vectors; left alone they turn toward their departure gate. The gates are real navaids in each direction: Badger and West Bend (north), Madison and Dells (northwest), Dubuque and Davenport (west), Peoria and Moline (southwest), Roberts and Champaign (south), Boiler and Brickyard (southeast), Gipper, Webster Lake and Fort Wayne (east) and Victory (northeast, for the Atlantic). They aren’t FAA-published gates; several navaids Chicago departures once used have been decommissioned.
  • Centers. Chicago Center (ZAU) owns the airspace around you; Minneapolis, Kansas City, Indianapolis and Cleveland Centers are beyond it.
  • Radars. The O’Hare and DuPage ASRs (C90’s own), plus Milwaukee, Rockford and South Bend.
  • Holds. 68 published holds, 59 of them on the Chicago STARs, for example WYNDE (253° inbound, right turns, 9 NM legs, 200 kt) and ENDEE (030° inbound, right turns, 10 NM legs, 200 kt).
  • Terrain and lake. O’Hare is at 680 ft, so minimum vectoring altitudes near the airports are higher than around JFK (2,700–3,300 ft). The video map shows the Lake Michigan shore east of the airports.
  • Weather. Live weather uses the O’Hare and Midway METARs. Random wind follows Chicago’s typical pattern: mostly south to southwest, then west and northwest, and less often northeast off the lake or southeast.

Dallas–Fort Worth

D10, the Dallas/Fort Worth TRACON, on the radio as Regional Approach and Regional Departure: DFW and Love Field, 10 NM apart, with Fort Worth Center around you.

DFW

Seven runways. In the usual south flow DFW lands on four at once (17C, 17L, 18R and the diagonal 13R) and departs 17R and 18L; in north flow it lands 35C, 35R, 36L and 31R and departs 35L and 36R. When the wind is too strong across some of them it drops to two arrival runways, or to one diagonal in a strong southeast or northwest wind. The tower is split: east-side runways (17C, 17L, 13L and their opposite ends) talk to Tower East on 126.55, the west side to Tower West on 124.15, as in the FAA’s data.

DFW is American’s biggest hub, with Envoy, SkyWest and PSA flying for American Eagle, plus Delta and United to their hubs, Frontier, Air Canada, Aeromexico and Volaris to Mexico, FedEx and UPS, and long-haul carriers to their own hubs: British Airways (A380 and 777) to London, Qantas’s A380 to Sydney, Emirates, Qatar, Lufthansa, Iberia, Japan Airlines and Korean Air.

Love Field

Southwest’s home, with Alaska to the West Coast and Delta to Atlanta and LaGuardia. Both of its parallel runways have an ILS: it lands 13L and departs 13R, or lands 31R and departs 31L.

What’s different

  • Departures climb via their SIDs. DFW and Love Field have 50 coded SIDs, most of them RNAV with transitions for each runway. A departure takes off on the SID that leads to its gate and checks in “Regional Departure, … climbing via the KATZZ three departure”, so you mostly let it fly, climb it higher, and keep it clear of the arrivals. The gates are fixes on those SIDs: ZEMMA, EAKER and ADM north, MLC northeast, ZACHH and TRYTN east, FORCK, MRSSH and BDDAY southeast, TORNN, ELLVR and WINDU south, ACT and HOARY southwest, ALIAN and WSTEX west, HUDAD, HRPER and KATZZ northwest.
  • Arrivals descend via. 25 of the 32 STARs publish restrictions, many with speeds (SEEVR4: SEEVR 13,000–16,000 ft at 270 kt, BRDJE at 250 kt, YAHBT at 6,000 ft and 230 kt).
  • Four finals at once. In south or north flow, arrivals are spread across four runways: two parallels each side of the airport and the diagonal.
  • Centers. Fort Worth Center (ZFW) owns the airspace around you; Houston, Kansas City and Memphis Centers are beyond it.
  • Radars. The DFW, Fort Worth naval air station and Greenville ASRs (D10’s own), plus Waco and Longview.
  • Holds. 88 published holds, 66 of them on the STARs, for example SEEVR (225° inbound, right turns, 10 NM legs, 230 kt) and BEREE (324° inbound, left turns, 10 NM legs, 230 kt).
  • Weather. Live weather uses the DFW and Love Field METARs. Random wind is mostly from the south, with north winds behind fronts, and less often east or west.

Reading the scope

The scope is fed by the region’s real radars. Targets step forward each time a radar that covers them sweeps past, rather than gliding, like a real scope.

Radar coverage

  • Terminal radars. Five airport surveillance radars: JFK, Newark, Islip and White Plains (the New York TRACON’s own) and Philadelphia. Each sweeps every 4.8 seconds, independently of the others, and sees about 60 NM, less for low aircraft because of the curve of the earth. Each draws its own sweep on the scope.
  • Long-range radar covers the whole region above 6,000 ft and updates every 12 seconds.
  • Coasting. Where no radar sees an aircraft (typically low and far from an airport), the sim keeps flying it but the scope freezes it at its last position with a # symbol and CST in its data block, until coverage picks it up again.
  • Turn on Radar sites in Map layers to see each antenna and its range. Coverage can be set to Everywhere on the setup screen if you prefer one radar that sees it all.

Data blocks

Each aircraft you control carries a data block. The Expanded style (default) looks like this:

  • Line 1: callsign.
  • Line 2: altitude in hundreds of feet (050 is 5,000 ft; 350 is FL350), a trend arrow (↑ climbing, ↓ descending), the assigned altitude while it is changing level, then the speed in knots. By default that is indicated airspeed, the speed the pilot flies and the one speed restrictions and your speed assignments use. You can switch to ground speed (what a real scope shows) under Settings → Display; it reads much higher at altitude because the air is thinner (about 450 kt at FL370 for 256 kt indicated).
  • Line 3: aircraft type, destination, and where it is navigating (→FIX or ILS22L). Nothing is shown there when it is flying a heading.

The STARS style is the real two-line format: speed in tens of knots, time-shared with type and destination. Switch styles in Layers or Settings.

Cleared for the ILS? Its target and data block fade, so the traffic that still needs you stands out. It is drawn at full strength again when you hover or select it, or when it is in a conflict. The fade can be turned off or adjusted in Settings → Display.

Data blocks move themselves to a clear side of their target when they would overlap. To place one yourself, use the 3×3 picker in the command panel; the center dot puts it back on automatic.

Trails and vectors

  • History trail: the last 8 radar returns as fading dots behind each target (up to 20).
  • Heat trail: each aircraft’s path since it became your traffic, drawn as a heat-colored line. Color it by age (hot where it is now), altitude (hot low, cool high) or speed (hot fast). Choose how much to keep, from 5 minutes to the whole path; the oldest part fades out.
  • Heading vector: a short line showing the direction each aircraft is pointing.
  • Selected route: select an aircraft to see the route it is flying, in its own color.

Tools

  • Drag to pan; scroll, pinch or double-click to zoom smoothly around the cursor. C recenters on the whole region (the default view size is a setting).
  • Right-drag to measure bearing and distance between two points.
  • The footer shows the cursor’s latitude and longitude.

Giving instructions

Click an aircraft (or its callsign in the radio log) to open its command panel. Build an instruction from one or more tabs, then press Transmit.

Under the aircraft’s altitude, speed and heading, planning chips help you think ahead: for arrivals, the distance to the airport and when to start down (turning amber when it’s time); for departures and overflights, the requested level (with a check once you’ve cleared it) and which Center they leave into, and how far that is.

Tab What it does
Heading Turn to a heading on the dial, left, right or the shortest way. A heading ends any procedure or direct-to (radar vectors).
Altitude Climb or descend, 2,000 ft up to the aircraft’s ceiling. Flight levels at and above 18,000 ft. The requested cruise level is marked REQ; levels below the minimum vectoring altitude where the aircraft is are flagged.
Descend via On the Altitude tab for an arrival on a STAR with published altitudes: fly the arrival’s restrictions down to its bottom altitude. Assigning an altitude cancels them (speed restrictions stay); Descend via gives them back.
Climb via On the Altitude tab for a departure on its SID: fly the departure’s restrictions up to the altitude it is cleared to. Pick an altitude as well for “climb via the PORTT four departure, except maintain flight level two three zero”, which keeps the restrictions with a new top altitude. An altitude on its own cancels them (speed restrictions stay).
Speed Assign a speed in 10 kt steps (never above 250 kt below 10,000 ft), or resume normal speed. An assigned speed overrides a procedure’s published speed limits; resuming normal speed brings them back.
Direct Fly direct to a fix. Fixes still ahead on its route come first, then the others in distance rings. Hover a fix to highlight it on the scope with its bearing and distance. Shortcut: with the aircraft selected, hold Ctrl (⌘ on a Mac) and click a fix on the scope; the fix under the cursor is highlighted while you hold it.
Hold Hold at a fix: fixes on its route first, then nearby fixes with a published hold (PUB). Hold as published (“hold at CAMRN as published, expect further clearance one four three two”) or as you describe it (inbound course and left or right turns), with an expect-further-clearance time 5–30 minutes ahead. While it holds on its STAR or SID, Resume sends it on along the procedure. See Holding.
Approach Clear an arrival for an ILS, runway in use first. See Arrivals for the rules.

You can combine tabs into one transmission, and it is read out in the order controllers say it (lateral, vertical, speed, clearance, handoff):

  • N90JetBlue twenty-two ninety-seven, turn left heading two five zero, descend and maintain one one thousand, reduce speed to two five zero knots.
  • JBU2297Left heading two five zero, descend and maintain one one thousand, reduce speed to two five zero knots, JetBlue twenty-two ninety-seven.

Pilots take 2–6 seconds to respond (a setting). The instruction shows as pending until they read it back, and they only start turning, climbing or slowing after that.

Arrivals

Working arrivals

Arrivals enter about 150 NM out on their STARs, at their cruise level or on a normal descent profile. Descending, sequencing and landing them is your job.

  1. Check-in. The pilot calls you with their level and STAR, for example “New York Center, United seventeen twenty-five, flight level two eight zero, PARCH four arrival, information Papa.” High traffic calls Center; lower traffic calls Approach. Both are you. The letter is the destination’s current ATIS (see Wind & runways).
  2. Descend via, or descend them. On a STAR that publishes altitude restrictions, arrivals check in “…descending via the PROUD two arrival” and fly them on their own, like an airliner’s FMS: they stay up until the top of descent, descend to meet each restriction at its fix, and level at the bottom (the last restriction). On other STARs they hold their altitude until you descend them. A good rule is 3 NM of track for every 1,000 ft to lose: from FL350 to 3,000 ft needs about 100 NM.
  3. Slow them down. They slow to 250 kt by 10,000 ft on their own, descending more slowly (or holding 10,000 ft) if you clear them down too fast to slow in time. Use speed control to space them on final.
  4. Vector to the final. Turn them onto an intercept heading for the localizer, at or below the glideslope and above the minimum vectoring altitude.
  5. Clear the ILS. Use the Approach tab. If the clearance doesn’t meet the criteria below, the pilot replies unable with the reason, and still follows the rest of the instruction. Once cleared, the aircraft fades on the scope.
  6. Tower takes over. Once the aircraft is established on the localizer and glideslope, it is handed to Tower automatically, slows for landing and lands. +100 RP.

Holding

When arrivals bunch up, hold one. Pick a fix in the Hold tab: 83 published holds from the FAA’s data are in Vector, 51 of them charted on the New York STARs (CAMRN: 041° inbound, left turns, 210 kt; BRAND: 060° inbound, right turns, 4 NM legs). The pilot flies to the fix and around a racetrack:

  • At the fix, a turn in the hold’s direction to the outbound heading (every entry is flown as a direct entry), the outbound leg (1 minute at or below 14,000 ft, 1½ minutes above, or the published distance), a turn back, and the inbound course tracked to the fix.
  • No faster than the published maximum or the standard holding speeds: 200 kt up to 6,000 ft, 230 kt to 14,000 ft, 265 kt above. Pilots slow down in the last 3 minutes before the fix.
  • At its expect-further-clearance time a pilot still holding calls: “…holding at CAMRN, we’re at our expect further clearance time, request further clearance.”
  • A heading, a direct-to or an approach clearance takes it out of the hold. Held at a fix on its STAR or SID, Resume continues the procedure from that fix; a hold stops a descent or climb via the procedure where it is (descend via resumes with it).
  • The data block shows HOLD CAMRN; select the aircraft to see its racetrack. Altitude and speed instructions work as usual while it holds, and the time spent holding counts against its target time.

ILS acceptance criteria

Check Rule (defaults)
Position In front of the runway, within 25 NM of the threshold.
Localizer coverage Within ±35° of the final course out to 18 NM, ±10° beyond.
Intercept angle No more than 60° to the final course, and converging with it.
Join point On its heading, the aircraft must join the localizer at least 5 NM from the runway. An aircraft still turning to its intercept heading is judged from where the turn will actually bring it in.
Altitude at the join Judged from the higher of its altitude and the altitude it is cleared to. At or below the glideslope, or high enough only to get down to it before the gate: what the type can descend at its speed beyond the glideslope’s own 318 ft per NM (little at 250 kt, more when slow), never less than 300 ft. Not below the minimum vectoring altitude.
Speed Able to slow from the join point to final approach speed before the stabilized-approach gate.

Pilots judge a clearance from where they will join the localizer on their heading, not from where they are when you clear them. An aircraft that joins above the glideslope descends to capture it from above, as real crews do. Pilots judge the clearance when you transmit it, with the rest of the instruction applied (an intercept heading given with it, say), so their answer is always what the Approach tab showed; the tab’s indication includes the heading you are giving.

After the clearance. Until it captures the localizer, a heading, altitude or speed you give it is judged against the approach. If that instruction is what stops the approach working, the pilot says so in the readback (“…two five zero, unable ILS runway two two from there, too high for the approach”), the clearance is cancelled and the aircraft is back on your vectors; the command panel warns you before you transmit. A cleared aircraft heading where it can no longer join the localizer reports “unable to intercept the localizer on this heading, request vectors”. A heading given once it is on the localizer breaks off the approach, as you intend.

Established means on the localizer (captured within about 0.5 NM of the centerline, with a lead turn when converging) and on the glideslope (captured from below, or from above as it descends to it).

At 1,000 ft above the runway (the stabilized-approach gate) the aircraft must be established, within 0.1 NM of the centerline, and no more than 10 kt above final approach speed. If not, it goes around and says why: not established on the localizer, not established on the glideslope, 600 ft high, too fast or not aligned with the runway. It flies the published missed approach and comes back to you (−25 RP). Re-sequence it and clear it again.

Watch the MVA. Near the airports, the TRACON’s minimum vectoring altitude chart applies; farther out, the Center’s minimum IFR altitudes. Turn on either layer in Map layers to see the sectors and their altitudes.
Departures

Working departures

Each airport has a departure queue. You choose when each flight goes, and on which runway.

  1. Release. In the Departures panel, pick an active departure runway for a waiting flight. Tower clears it for takeoff; it lines up and rolls 30–50 seconds later. Takeoffs on the same runway are spaced automatically.
  2. Climb-out. It flies its SID toward its departure gate (or runway heading where no SID serves the runway), climbing to the airport’s initial altitude. Pilots slow down ahead of time for speed limits on the SID, so they reach each restricted fix at or below its speed. Tower owns it until then.
  3. Radar contact. Passing 1,500 ft it checks in with you and turns blue, for example “New York Departure, United two forty-seven, one thousand five hundred, climbing via the PORTT four departure.” Departures on a SID are cleared to climb via it, to the airport’s initial altitude: they never climb above an “at or below” restriction before its fix, and meet “at or above” ones by climbing normally (the New York SIDs publish only “at or above” restrictions, such as KIWIE 025+ and VOBOZ 045+ on the GLDMN8). From here it is your traffic.
  4. Climb to the requested level. Every departure files a cruise level for its trip (Requests FL360 in its command panel, REQ in the altitude menu). Short hops file lower, long hauls higher, and eastbound flights file odd levels (FL350) while westbound file even (FL360).
  5. Hand off to Center within 20 NM of the N90 boundary, at or above 17,000 ft eastbound or FL180 westbound. +40 RP, plus 30 for having flown its gate fix and 30 for being cleared to its requested level (up to +100 RP).

If a queue fills up, new departures wait at the gate until space opens. Queue size is part of the traffic settings.

Overflights

Working overflights

Overflights cross your airspace between cities elsewhere, at the level they filed (about FL160–FL410).

  1. Check-in. They enter on the boundary, flying direct to an exit fix on the far side, and call you at their cruise level.
  2. Keep them clear of your climbing departures and descending arrivals. They are often the traffic that crosses your flows.
  3. Hand off to the Center they are leaving into, within 20 NM of the N90 boundary, at or above 17,000 ft eastbound or FL180 westbound. +30 RP, plus 30 for having flown their exit fix and 30 at their requested level (up to +90 RP).

Handoffs and Center

Four Centers surround you: New York, Boston, Washington and Cleveland. The Handoff tab always offers the one the aircraft is flying into, on its nearest radio site for its altitude.

  • N90American seven eighty-seven, contact Washington Center one three three point one two five.
  • AAL787Washington Center one three three point one two five, good day, American seven eighty-seven.

When Center accepts a handoff

  • Near the N90 boundary: within 20 NM of where its heading takes it out of the N90 TRACON (the dashed outline on the scope). An aircraft already outside the TRACON can be handed off at once.
  • High enough: at or above 17,000 ft eastbound (magnetic heading 000–179°) or FL180 westbound (180–359°), and never below the FAA minimum vectoring or minimum IFR altitude where it is and where it will leave. (Real handoff altitudes are set in agreements between facilities that aren’t published; both values are settings.)
  • Leaving: heading out of your airspace. Arrivals are never handed to Center.

The Handoff tab shows each condition with a check mark, how far there is to go, and the RP on offer. Until the conditions are met, the handoff can’t be transmitted.

Flying the route

Pilots fly SIDs and STARs like an FMS: they start the turn before a fly-by fix so they roll out on the next leg, instead of overflying it and coming back.

Departures fly their SID and then proceed to their departure gate fix, including SIDs that end in “expect vectors” if you don’t vector them. Past the gate, they head on toward their destination. Overflights fly direct to their exit fix, then on toward their destination. Passing the gate or exit fix, on their own or on your vectors and direct-tos, earns the route bonus at the handoff.

After the readback the aircraft turns grey: it belongs to Center now. The computer Center controller then:

  • climbs or descends it to its requested cruise level and cancels any speed you assigned;
  • routes it along the rest of its flight plan and on toward its destination (the data block shows →KBOS);
  • keeps its own aircraft apart: when two are predicted to meet, one levels off 1,000 ft short or steps 2,000 ft, then continues once they are clear.
Your part: Center separates its traffic from each other, not from yours. A handed-off aircraft still counts in conflict alerts against your traffic, so hand off once it is clear of your flows.

Separation rules

Two aircraft are separated when they are far enough apart laterally or vertically. Only pairs involving your traffic are checked, and aircraft below 400 ft above the airport are Tower’s concern.

Where Minimum
Vertical, everywhere 1,000 ft
Lateral, within 40 NM of the JFK radar (terminal area) 3 NM
Lateral, when either aircraft is beyond 40 NM (en route) 5 NM
In trail on the same final, both inside 10 NM of the runway 2.5 NM

Wake turbulence on final

An aircraft following a heavier one on the same runway’s approach must stay farther behind it than the radar minimum, at any altitude, because the leader’s wake sinks along the glidepath (FAA Order 7110.65, 5-5-4). This is watched even after Tower has taken both aircraft, since the spacing comes from your sequencing.

Leader → follower Minimum
Heavy → heavy 4 NM
Heavy → B757 or large 5 NM
Heavy → small 6 NM
B757 → B757 or large 4 NM
B757 → small 5 NM
Large → small 4 NM
Super → super / heavy / B757 or large / small 4 / 6 / 7 / 8 NM

Heavies in the fleet are the A330, 767, 777 and 787; the 757 has its own category. A lost wake spacing shows WK over the data block and WAKE 3.8/5 NM on the line between the pair, with a single softer tone. Give heavies extra room when you sequence, and remember the leader slows to its final approach speed near the runway, so the gap behind it closes.

Conflict Alert looks 40 seconds ahead. An amber CA means separation will be lost if nothing changes. A flashing red CA with a repeating tone means it is lost now, and the pair is logged in the Alerts tab of the radio log.

RP scoring

RP (reputation points) rewards moving traffic safely and efficiently. Your running total is in the top bar; press R for the breakdown and latest events. Each change pops up as a notification.

Departure handed to Center +40
Overflight handed to Center +30

Bonus: handed off cleared to its requested level +30

Go-around (approach not stabilized) −25

Wake turbulence spacing lost behind a heavier aircraft on final −80 to −160

  • Separation of 5 NM or more is never penalized. A loss closer than that costs 150 RP, rising to 300 RP the closer the aircraft came. It is scored when the loss ends, at the closest point reached.
  • A near midair collision is scored the moment it happens and replaces the loss-of-separation penalty for that encounter.
  • These values are the same for every pilot: they’re set by Vector’s admins, not in your settings, so the records compare like with like. So are the rules: separation minima, wake turbulence, approach criteria, Center and radar.

Target times

Every flight gets a target time the moment it becomes yours, like the times arrival metering assigns in real life:

Flight Clock starts Target Allowance
Arrival When it enters Land by 10 min
Departure Radar contact (passing 1,500 ft) Handed to Center by 3 min
Overflight When it enters Handed to Center by 3 min

The target is the flight’s unimpeded time along its own route, plus 10% and the allowance above for sequencing. Vector works it out from the aircraft type’s speeds at each altitude: an arrival flies its STAR, joins the final of a runway in use 12 NM out and slows down along it; a departure flies its SID to its gate and on to where Center can take it, and never sooner than it can climb to the handoff altitude; an overflight crosses at its level to the handoff point. Efficient handling uses about 70–85% of the target; long vectors, holding for spacing or a go-around use up the rest.

  • The command panel shows the selected flight’s target, e.g. Land by 14:32Z · 6:12 left, amber in the last 2 minutes and red once it is late.
  • The RP panel (R) shows, for arrivals, departures and overflights, how many you have finished, your average time, your average against the target (−3:59 is ahead) and your on-time rate, plus the flights due next.
  • A flight that leaves without a handoff has no time scored (it loses 100 RP instead). Target times can be turned off, and every value is a setting.
  • Your session RP is saved with the session. The start screen shows each saved session’s RP and your career across your saved sessions: total RP, flights landed and handed off, on-time rate, losses of separation, wake spacing lost and go-arounds.

Session debrief

After you save, and before you leave the scope (click the Vector logo), a debrief shows how the session went: total RP with flights landed and handed off, the on-time rate and losses; RP over the session as a chart (hover, or focus it and use the arrow keys, for the value at any time; the same numbers as a table); how RP was earned and lost; timing per kind of flight; your best and worst flights (the worst with what cost them most); and the closest losses of separation. Leaving offers to save first. The sim pauses while the debrief is open.

Wind and runways

Each airport has its own wind, and the wind picks the runways, as it does in real life. Aircraft take off and land into the wind.

ATIS. Each airport broadcasts an ATIS in the style of a digital ATIS, for example JFK ATIS INFO C 0051Z. 14006KT 10SM FEW045 BKN250 18/16 A3010. ILS RUNWAYS 13L AND 22L APPROACHES IN USE. LANDING RUNWAYS 13L, 22L. DEPARTING RUNWAY 13R. It moves to the next letter when a new weather report arrives, when the runways change, when the wind changes by 30° or 5 kt from the broadcast, or after an hour; between letters the broadcast wind stays as issued, as on a real ATIS. The top bar shows each airport’s letter next to its name (hover for the broadcasts), the Departures panel shows it per airport, a notice says when a new one is current, and arrivals report the letter they have when they check in.

Live weather. In the Live wind mode (the default) Vector uses the real current METARs for your airports from the Aviation Weather Center, and checks for a new report every 5 minutes during the session (a setting); airports issue one about hourly, and special reports when the weather changes. Winds are shown magnetic, as on an ATIS, and gusts as reported. The top bar marks the wind LIVE; hover over it for each airport’s report. If a check fails the badge turns amber and the last reports stay in use (or a realistic wind, if none has arrived yet). A new report can bring a runway change like any other wind shift. The wind doesn’t drift on its own in Live mode.

Of the configurations that keep tailwind at 5 kt or less and crosswind at 20 kt or less on every runway, Vector picks the one facing most into the wind. An airport’s preferred configuration wins only when it gives up a little headwind (a quarter of the wind speed, at most 3 kt), and never when it would mean a tailwind while another runway has a headwind, so even a light wind is landed into: 030° at 4 kt puts JFK on the 4s. Only a calm wind (2 kt or less) uses the preferred runways regardless. If nothing is within limits, it picks the configuration that goes least over the tailwind limit, then the crosswind limit. On the setup screen you can override the choice per airport.

Dual operations at JFK. When the wind allows it on every runway, JFK runs two arrival runways (or two departure runways) at once, as it does in real life. Arrivals are spread across both, and the Departures panel offers each departure runway in use. In a strong crosswind for the second runway, JFK drops back to one of each.

The wind changes during a session. It drifts smoothly around its starting value, the airports moving together as one weather system: with Slight variation (default) by up to 20° and 4 kt, with Moderate by up to 40° and 8 kt, one swing taking about 20 minutes. Reported winds change in 10° and 1 kt steps, like an ATIS. Set the variation to Off for a steady wind. The variation settings are on the setup screen under Wind & runways. The top bar shows the regional wind with an arrow pointing where it blows; hover over it for each airport’s wind.

Gusts. A gusting wind reads like 220° 14G26 kt: 14 kt steady, peaks of 26. Gusts are only reported when the peaks are at least 10 kt above the steady wind, as in a real weather report. They come and go as the wind drifts, and crosswind limits are judged at the gust. Manual wind has its own gust setting.

Runway changes. When the wind drifts far enough that an airport’s runways go over the tailwind or crosswind limit, or they have a tailwind while other runways would get a clear headwind (at least 5 kt more), the airport announces a runway change: a notice pops up, and the Departures panel counts down to it with the new runways. After the notice (10 minutes by default) the new runways are in use. Aircraft already cleared for an approach to the old runway still land there; clear everyone else for the new runways. An airport keeps its runways for at least 30 minutes after a change, and runways you picked yourself on the setup screen are never changed.

Only runways in use get final approach course lines on the scope.

Difficulty sets the session’s traffic, and it stays the same throughout, so sessions on the records are comparable. The Traffic panel (T) shows it. Fine-tuned rates show as Custom: fine for practice, not ranked.

Arrival and departure rates are per airport, so Normal brings 30 arrivals an hour into the three airports. Each airport has its own real carriers, types and city pairs, and each airline uses its real radio name. Heavy aircraft add “heavy” to their callsign and the A380 adds “super” (“Emirates two zero one super”), in your transmissions and in their readbacks; a 757 doesn’t.

  • Kennedy: JetBlue, Delta, American, United and Alaska, the regionals, and long-haul carriers to their hubs only: British Airways and Virgin Atlantic to London, Air France to Paris, Lufthansa to Frankfurt and Munich, KLM to Amsterdam, Iberia to Madrid, Emirates (A380 and 777) to Dubai and Qatar to Doha.
  • LaGuardia: narrow-bodies and regional jets only (Delta’s A220s, Southwest, Frontier, Air Canada, Envoy, PSA, SkyWest and others), within the perimeter.
  • Newark: United’s hub, from E175s to 777s, plus FedEx to Memphis and Indianapolis, UPS to Louisville, British Airways to London and TAP to Lisbon.

Each type only flies trips within its practical airline range: CRJ900 1,500 NM, E175 1,800, A220, A320 and 737 (and their neo and MAX versions) about 2,700–2,900, A321neo LR 4,000, 757 3,900, wide-bodies 4,900–8,700. So transatlantic flights are wide-bodies and JetBlue’s and TAP’s A321neo, never regional jets or other narrow-bodies. Overflights are city pairs whose route really crosses New York, such as Boston–Atlanta or Montreal–Florida. The airline mix is Realistic (each airport’s real share) or Varied (smaller carriers more often).

Saving and resuming

  • Press Shift S or Save. The sim pauses, and you name the session. The debrief follows; keep working or go back to the start screen. A session you loaded or saved before can be overwritten or saved as a new copy.
  • Saved sessions appear on the start screen with their traffic level, sim time, aircraft count and RP. Resume, rename or delete them there.
  • A resumed session continues exactly where you saved it, paused, including traffic settings, heat trails, Center’s plans and your RP.
  • You can keep up to 25 saved sessions on your account.

Map layers (M)

  • Coastline and rivers, runways, airport labels
  • The region’s other airports (PHL, ISP, HPN, TEB, BDL and more)

  • Fixes and navaids (plain waypoints hide when zoomed out)

  • Minimum vectoring altitudes and Center minimum IFR altitudes
  • An optional real-world map underneath

Settings (O)

  • Display: brightness, data block style and size, trails, heat trails, colors, range rings, sweep, scope range, UI animation
  • Audio: volume, Conflict Alert tones, interface sounds
  • Controls: zoom sensitivity, direct-to rings, keyboard shortcuts

These follow your account to any device. Session rules (separation, approaches, pilots, Center, RP) are set on the setup screen and saved with each session.

Controller tips

  • Plan descents early. Arrivals enter high; three miles per thousand feet tells you when to start.
  • Use speed before vectors. Small speed changes 40 NM out save big turns later.
  • Intercept at 20–30° when you can (up to 60° is accepted), below the glideslope, and at least 5 NM from the threshold.
  • Separate crossing flows vertically. Keep departures climbing under or over overflights by at least 1,000 ft until they have passed.
  • Climb departures to their requested level and let them fly their gate fix before the handoff for both bonuses. Hand off in the last 20 NM before the N90 boundary so you never lose 100 RP.
  • Watch the amber CA. It gives you 40 seconds to fix a conflict before it costs RP.
  • Mind the clock. Check the RP panel’s Due next list: a flight a minute from its target is worth a shortcut (a direct-to or an earlier handoff) if the traffic allows it.
  • Pause freely. Planning while paused is how real controllers wish they could work.
